## Problem Statement

Tetanggaku faced a challenging market condition: entering an already-saturated ride-hailing industry dominated by established players (Gojek, Grab) with significantly greater resources. The challenge was to:

- **Establish competitive positioning** against well-funded incumbents
- **Design differentiated user experiences** with a lean startup team and budget constraints
- **Scale product design** with minimal design resources (one designer)
- **Build a cohesive ecosystem** across two-sided platforms (consumers and drivers)

**The constraint:** Operate without traditional product infrastructure (formal PRDs, design systems) while maintaining high-quality UX.

---

## Competitive Landscape & Research

### Competitive Analysis
One-week analysis of leading ride-hailing platforms to understand established patterns and best practices.

- **Scope:** Gojek and Grab (the two market leaders in Southeast Asia)
- **Focus:** Key user flows and UI patterns
- **Method:** Flow mapping, pattern documentation, interaction analysis
- **Key learnings:** Service diversity, simplified onboarding, payment flexibility, driver incentive design

### Strategic Approach
Rather than create entirely original designs, the strategy focused on thoughtful adaptation — taking proven patterns from market leaders and tailoring them to Tetanggaku's positioning and constraints.

---

## Design Process

### Phase 1: Requirement Definition Through Verbal Collaboration
- **Method:** Direct conversation-based briefs with PM and CEO
- **Challenge:** No formal Product Requirements Documents (PRDs)
- **Adaptation:** Maintained comprehensive project notes and requirement clarifications through conversations
- **Outcome:** Agile requirement gathering despite lack of formal documentation

### Phase 2: Stakeholder Brainstorming
Collaborative sessions with PM, CEO, and CTO to:

- Define feature scope and priorities
- Identify critical user flows
- Align on business constraints and technical feasibility
- Establish design direction and brand personality

**Key decisions made:** Service offerings (motorcycle, car, delivery), driver incentive structures, payment models.

### Phase 3: High-Fidelity Design (No Low-Fidelity Phase)
**Constraint:** Limited timeline required moving directly to high-fidelity design, skipping wireframing and low-fidelity prototyping.

**Adaptation:** Applied deep competitive research and interaction patterns to inform high-fidelity design decisions directly.

- **Process:** Sketch concept → High-fidelity design in Figma → Stakeholder presentation
- **Scope:** Complete user flows, feature walkthroughs, edge cases
- **Outcome:** Delivered production-ready design specifications without intermediate wireframing steps

### Phase 4: Iterative Presentation & Refinement
Presented designs with full journey walkthroughs and feature narratives to the team:

- Demonstrated user flows across service types (motorcycle, car, delivery)
- Showed driver and consumer side-by-side journeys
- Incorporated feedback and refined designs for implementation
- Maintained design consistency across dual-sided experience

## Design Decisions

### 1. Service Diversification (Motorcycle, Car, Delivery)
**Decision:** Design distinct but cohesive experiences for three service types within a single ecosystem.

**Rationale:** Expanded addressable market (short vs. long distance, consumer vs. item delivery). Competitive differentiation from single-service focused competitors.

### 2. Seamless Two-Sided Experience
**Decision:** Maintain design consistency and interaction patterns across customer and driver apps despite different use cases.

**Rationale:** Builds unified ecosystem identity. Reduces design complexity when both user types interact within the same system.

### 3. Simplified Onboarding
**Decision:** Minimize friction in driver signup and customer account creation to accelerate user acquisition.

**Rationale:** Critical in competitive market. Every step in onboarding reduces conversion. Simplified process essential for rapid growth.

### 4. Competitive Feature Parity
**Decision:** Implement established features from Gojek/Grab (tracking, ratings, payments) rather than inventing new patterns.

**Rationale:** Users expect certain features; reinventing them adds risk without differentiation benefit. Focus innovation on business model or service quality, not interaction patterns.

### 5. Driver Incentive Transparency
**Decision:** Make earnings, referral bonuses, and performance metrics visible and easy to understand.

**Rationale:** Driver motivation and retention depend on understanding earning potential. Transparent dashboards reduce driver churn and improve service quality perception.

## Design Challenges & Solutions

### Challenge 1: Designing Without Formal Requirements
**Problem:** No written PRDs; requirements evolved through conversation.

**Solution:** Maintained detailed conversation notes and created design documentation that served as the source of truth. Design specs became pseudo-PRDs for development.

### Challenge 2: One Designer, Multiple Service Types
**Problem:** Sole designer responsible for cohesive experience across motorcycle, car, and delivery services.

**Solution:** Developed reusable interaction patterns and component-level consistency principles. Applied "constraint-based design"—strict rules ensured consistency across services despite rapid iteration.

### Challenge 3: Competing with Well-Funded Incumbents
**Problem:** Gojek and Grab had orders of magnitude more resources for design and product.

**Solution:** Focused on UX fundamentals (clarity, simplicity, speed) rather than trying to out-feature competitors. Emphasized service quality and driver experience as differentiators.

### Challenge 4: High-Fidelity-First Design
**Problem:** No wireframing phase meant higher risk of design rework if flows were misaligned.

**Solution:** Deep competitive research and stakeholder alignment before design ensured high-fidelity designs were directionally correct. Reduced rework through upfront validation.

## Key Learnings

### 1. Constraints Drive Innovation in Process
The absence of formal PRDs and wireframing forced a more iterative, conversation-based approach. This actually accelerated decision-making by eliminating layers of documentation.

### 2. Design Consistency Scales Across Complexity
One designer managing multiple service types (motorcycle, car, delivery) was possible because of rigorous consistency principles. Constraint-based design rules ensured quality at scale.

### 3. Competitive Parity is a Feature, Not a Weakness
In saturated markets, implementing proven patterns better than competitors matters more than inventing new ones. Quality execution of expected features beats mediocre innovation.

### 4. Driver Experience is a Design Differentiator
In ride-hailing, driver quality directly impacts customer experience. Investing in driver UX (incentive transparency, earnings clarity) was more impactful than marginal customer-side features.

### 5. High-Fidelity-First Can Work With Proper Validation
Skipping wireframing was risky but workable because stakeholder alignment and competitive research validated the direction before high-fidelity design began.

### 6. Scale Efficiency Through Patterns, Not Processes
Rather than building elaborate design systems or processes, reusable interaction patterns and strict consistency principles achieved scalability with minimal overhead.

## Reflection

This project demonstrated that **design excellence can enable market competition despite resource constraints**. By focusing on UX fundamentals, applying one week of competitive research thoughtfully, and maintaining consistency across a complex, two-sided platform, a single designer enabled Tetanggaku to achieve 100K+ downloads and 4.5-star ratings (2,240 reviews) within the first year of launch.

The work also highlighted that **startup constraints, while challenging, can drive process innovation**. The absence of formal PRDs and low-fidelity phases forced a more conversational, stakeholder-aligned approach to design. While unconventional, this approach reduced overhead and accelerated decision-making.

Finally, the project reinforced that in saturated markets, **service quality and UX consistency matter as much as feature innovation**. Tetanggaku's ability to compete wasn't about inventing ride-hailing, but about executing the established model with attention to user and driver experience.
